ARCHIVAL IDENTIFICATION TRIAGE

Before an unidentified reel is discarded, investigate what it might be.

Last Seen Alive finds clues inside a nameless film fragment, searches historical records, tries to prove itself wrong, and shows an archivist exactly what the evidence can—and cannot—establish.

A five-role Google ADK workflow extracts typed multimodal clues, researches through six Parallel surfaces, re-opens every cited page to confirm the quotation, attempts falsification, then passes structured evidence to a pure deterministic gate with mandatory human approval.

WHO THIS IS FOR

An archivist triaging unidentified material.

The tool does the broad, repetitive investigation and hands over a dossier. The archivist—not the model—decides whether the evidence is strong enough to act on.

The gate requires three independent domains, two distinct clue families, temporal and entity compatibility, no unresolved contradiction, cited decisive claims, and explicit human approval.

THE SCALE, FROM THE PEOPLE WHO COUNTED IT

Most of it is already gone. What survives is often nameless.

The Library of Congress commissioned a census. Of roughly 11,000 American silent feature films, 14 percent — about 1,575 titles — survive in their original format. Another 26 percent of survivors were found abroad.

Survival is not the whole problem. A reel that exists but cannot be named is not catalogued, not searchable and not prioritised for funding. The Library runs an annual workshop for exactly this, and it publishes its own hit rate.

“Mostly Lost”, Library of Congress Packard Campus — films identified during the event. Further identifications follow afterwards: 46 more in 2017, 24 more in 2018.

This is context, not a scoreboard. Their corpus is 187 films chosen because they are hard; ours is five with a sealed answer key. The two are not comparable, five cases support no rate at all, and this project makes no claim to outperform expert archivists. It is cited so a reader knows that the people who do this for a living identify well under half of what they screen — and that anyone promising near-certainty on this task is selling something. HOW IT WORKS

Five roles research. Code verifies. A human decides.

  1. 01

    Visual Examiner

    Writes down the words and physical clues visible in the fragment.

    Gemini multimodal emits a typed clue set; verbatim intertitles remain verbatim.

  2. 02

    Phrase Hunter

    Searches old publications using the rarest exact words.

    Parallel Search API receives two to five literal, diverse probes and returns long cited excerpts.

  3. 03

    Holdings Researcher

    Checks alternate titles, then counts which archives actually list the film.

    Parallel Task API performs multi-hop research against a JSON output schema; Parallel FindAll enumerates the named catalogues that hold it.

  4. 04

    Skeptic

    Tries to prove each candidate wrong.

    Gemini directs fresh Parallel searches for date, entity and attribution contradictions.

  5. 05

    Citation audit · deterministic

    Every page a claim quotes is re-opened and checked.

    Parallel Extract fetches each cited page; a quotation that is not in the live document is refused by the gate.

  6. 06

    Evidence gate · deterministic

    Code checks every rule. An archivist must still approve.

    A pure Python function exposes every boolean threshold as gate.passed and gate.failed.

WHAT IT WILL NOT DO

Uncertainty is an output.

It never says “last surviving copy.” Searching every catalogue you can name tells you where a print is; it can never tell you no other print exists. The only permitted sentence is: “No additional holding was found across these named catalogues as of this search date” — followed by the names. Sentences that break that rule are caught in code and shown to you as violations rather than published as findings.

A fragment without legible, discriminating clues may remain unidentified, and the system will abstain. That is a result, not a failure — and you can leave a standing watch on it.

Two results carry it. D02: the control returned three different Bray cartoons across three runs, every one at high confidence, none sourced, all wrong. The full system returned Dud Leaves Home (1919) — the answer key exactly — on 9 decisive claims across 9 domains, citing the very Library of Congress record the benchmark was built from. Then we ran it 28 times, and the column above did not survive

Arm C is a single pass. Six passes over the same five fragments — 28 runs in total — say something quite different, and we would rather you heard it from us.

Two findings hurt. D02 returned four different leading candidates in four runs — the correct film once, the studio once, and two other films. And D04 returned the same wrong film, Un coin de Paris (1900), in three of four runs at five of seven thresholds: not noise, a reproducible misidentification. “Zero false-confident identifications” is a property of one pass, not of this system, and every surface that quoted it now says so.

We fixed both defects and measured again, and only one of them went away. Candidates that named no film went from 7 to 0. False-confident identifications did not improve — constraining the candidate type turned disguised failures into visible wrong answers rather than removing them. The error rate was always this high; the non-films were hiding it. Both eras are published at /v1/eval/fix-comparison.

We could have shipped the good pass and said nothing. Publishing this is the only position consistent with everything else on this page. And one thing did hold across all 19: no run ever reached probable, because that threshold needs a human the API cannot supply. Every wrong answer above arrived as candidates with its failing thresholds attached. The defensible claim is not that this system is not wrong — it is that it does not assert what it cannot support, and shows its working.

The miss, and why we are keeping it

D04 abstained where the answer key says Buying a cow. The research worked — 13 domains, “LOUVRE” and “TABAC” correctly transcribed. Then ten citations were rejected: eight because the compiler paraphrased instead of quoting Parallel verbatim, two because Extract could not find the quote on the live page. No decisive claim survived, so it abstained. The strictness that produces zero false-confident identifications is the same strictness that cost this one. We are not loosening it; a system that accepts paraphrased citations has given up the only thing it offers.

PRIOR ART

Why not a screenshot matcher or archive catalogue?

Our documented search found screenshot identifiers, archive cataloguing products, and crowdsourced identification projects, but did not find a production system combining multimodal clue extraction, live open-web historical investigation, adversarial verification, global holdings research, and claim-level provenance. This is a documented search result, not a claim that private systems cannot exist.

HONEST RISKS

The benchmark is easier than a genuinely unknown reel.

The evaluation fragments have known answers; real orphan material may not. Search coverage is uneven outside digitised English-language sources. Exact phrases can be reused. Human approval is mandatory because the gate measures evidence completeness, not historical truth.

Live stack

Google Cloud is probed live. The Parallel probe costs a real search, so a healthy result is cached for fifteen minutes and a failure for one.
